Why the matches find π

Buffon's needle (1777), worked through step by step, from my notes for Alexandru Negrescu's course Mathematics for Understanding AI. The panel under the big number above is the picture behind step 3.

  1. The experiment

    Lines are drawn a distance \(d\) apart and the matches have length \(\ell \le d\); here \(\ell = d\). For a dropped match, let \(x\) be the distance from its middle to the nearest line and \(\theta\) the angle it makes with the lines. A fair drop makes them independent and uniform:

    $$x \sim \mathcal{U}\!\left[0, \tfrac{d}{2}\right], \qquad \theta \sim \mathcal{U}\!\left[0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$$

    chibrituri aruncate la întâmplare pe o podea cu linii paralele

  2. When does it cross?

    The match reaches \(\tfrac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta\) above and below its middle, so it lands across a line exactly when that reach is at least the distance to the nearest line:

    $$\text{crosses} \iff x \le \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta$$

  3. Probability is an area

    The pair \((\theta, x)\) is uniform on a box of area \(\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\). The crossing drops are the ones under the curve \(x = \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta\), so

    $$\begin{aligned} P(\text{cross}) &= \frac{1}{\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{d}{2}}\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta \, d\theta \\ &= \frac{4}{\pi d}\cdot\frac{\ell}{2}\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{2\ell}{\pi d} \end{aligned}$$

    That is the panel under the big number: every match becomes a dot at \((\theta, x)\), the gold ones sit under the sine curve, and the gold share of the box heads to \(2/\pi \approx 63.66\%\).

    probabilitatea = aria de sub curbă ÷ aria dreptunghiului

  4. Turn it around

    With \(\ell = d\) the crossing probability is \(2/\pi\). Drop \(n\) matches and count \(C\) crossings. By the strong law of large numbers \(C/n \to 2/\pi\) almost surely, so

    $$\hat\pi_n = \frac{2n}{C} \;\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\; \pi$$

    That's the big number above: two times the drops, divided by the crossings.

  5. No π hidden inside

    Picking \(\theta\) uniformly from \([0, \pi/2]\) would need π, which would be cheating. Instead, the simulation picks a point \((a, b)\) uniformly in the square \([-1, 1]^2\), throws it away unless \(a^2 + b^2 \le 1\), and points the match along \((a, b)\). Points inside a circle face every direction equally. With \(\sin\theta = |b|/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) and \(\ell = 1\), the crossing test becomes

    $$x^2\,(a^2 + b^2) \le \tfrac14\, b^2$$

    which only needs multiplication. π appears only when drawing the reference line and the 0°–90° axis, never in the estimate.

    side note: the share of points kept is π/4, which is another way to estimate π

  6. How close should it get?

    The estimate is 2 divided by a sample proportion, so its typical error is about \(\frac{\pi^2}{2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \approx 2.37/\sqrt{n}\) with \(p = 2/\pi\):

    matches10010,0001 million100 million
    typical error±0.24±0.024±0.0024±0.00024

    Each extra correct decimal costs 100 times more matches. After 100 million matches, expect around four correct decimals.

  7. Lazzarini's lucky throw

    In 1901 Mario Lazzarini reported 3,408 drops with \(\ell/d = 5/6\) and 1,808 crossings:

    $$\begin{aligned} \hat\pi &= \frac{2\ell}{d}\cdot\frac{n}{C} = \frac{5}{3}\cdot\frac{3408}{1808} \\ &= \frac{355}{113} = 3.1415929\ldots \end{aligned}$$

    That is correct to six decimals. With 3,408 drops the typical error is about ±0.05, so landing that close by chance is extremely unlikely. 355/113 is a famous fraction for π, and most likely the experiment was stopped, or the counts chosen, to land on it.
